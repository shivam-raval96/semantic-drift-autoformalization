Task-pair fine-tuning: does the learned skill survive a different rigid grammar?

Harsh Soni · ft-experiments/v2, branch harsh/experiments · August 2026

Experiment

Follow-up to the grammar-only run, built around the suggestion to train on the pairs and then test on a different rigid grammar. Fine-tune on story→grammar pairs written in ONE notation, then ask the same frozen problems in notations the model never trained on. If the model learned to translate, the skill should survive the change of notation; if it only learned a format, it should not.

Three output grammars, ordered by how far they sit from the trained one: A (trained): ASSUME:/ASK: with op(a, b). B-near: same structure, different words and symbol — GIVEN:/SHOW: with f(a, b). B-far: different structure — LAW:/DERIVE: with parenthesized infix (a ∘ b). Each is specified in-context in its own frozen prompt; each is graded mechanically by parsing it back to the same equation trees the original grader uses, so nothing about scoring changes between grammars.

Setup

Trained on2,772 story→answer pairs in grammar A only (plus a 100-pair holdout); one story theme held out of training entirely
Tested oneval_v1: 777 frozen problems, 4 difficulty tiers — equations provably disjoint from training under the grader's symmetry group
ModelsLlama-3.1-8B, Ministral-3-14B, Qwen3-32B (LoRA r=16 all layers, loss on the answer only, 3 epochs, greedy decoding)
Input armsstory and literal-description — only story was trained, so the literal arm is a second free generalization test
Controlsbase model on every cell; unrelated format-following suite; checkpoint-by-checkpoint curves; internal probes

Figure 1. Blue = after fine-tuning, gray = base. Flat and high across the trained grammar and the re-skinned one, then a cliff at the restructured one.

Answers in a never-trained grammar are as good as in the trained one, as long as the structure is the same (B-near within a few points of A everywhere), and the same holds for the literal input arm, which never appeared in training either. The restructured grammar is where it breaks — but even there every model gains 25–65 points over base.

Result 2: the remaining gap is a capacity limit, and it is syntactic

B-far performance scales sharply with model size: ~37% (8B) → ~50% (14B) → ~80% (32B). And the failures are not misunderstandings. On the deepest tier the 14B scores 97% in grammar A but 24% in B-far — and of its 152 failures there, 119 are unbalanced parentheses and only 5 are semantically wrong. The model knows the answer and drops the syntax while writing an unfamiliar notation. Larger models stop making that mistake.

Result 3: transfer peaks early, then training specializes it away

Figure 2. Same models across training. Blue = trained grammar, orange = never-trained restructured grammar, evaluated at every saved checkpoint.

The two curves rise together — the never-trained grammar improves as the trained skill improves, which is the opposite of format lock-in. But in the smaller models the orange curve then comes back down: the 14B is level with grammar A at step 100 (61% vs 61%), peaks at 74.5% at step 300, and decays to 59% by the end while grammar A keeps climbing. Continued training trades generality for specialization — and that trade disappears at 32B, where both curves hold flat to the end. Practical consequence: the final checkpoint is not the most transferable one, and an endpoint-only experiment would have under-reported 14B transfer by ~15 points.

Reusing the probing instruments: the internal correctness signal, which grammar-only fine-tuning left completely untouched (8B stayed at 0.52), is installed by task fine-tuning in all three models. It holds at full strength on a subset restricted to laws the fine-tuned model provably never saw. For scale, the strongest signal ever measured in a model we did not train was 0.815.

What this changes

  • Grammar was never the bottleneck; the task was. Same equations, same models, same recipe as the first run — the only change is whether the task itself was in the training data. Before: notation perfected, meaning unchanged or destroyed (32B fell 34% → 3%). Now: 32B goes 34% → 99.9%, and transfers.
  • What gets learned is a skill with a real boundary. It survives new words, new symbols, and a new input format; it partially survives a new tree syntax, and that boundary moves outward with model size.
  • Fine-tuning is not just behavior. Behavior, internal representation, and general instruction-following can now be measured separately — the format-following control was intact for every model, so nothing here is a model that merely got narrower.

Checks behind these numbers

All 19,425 stored verdicts across every base and fine-tuned run were independently re-graded from the raw model outputs by auditors that were told to trust nothing — exact match, with row-order and summary arithmetic re-derived. Training/evaluation disjointness was recomputed from scratch; the training prompts were verified byte-identical to what the evaluation sends; the new grammar graders were fuzz-tested for equal leniency with the original so no grammar is scored more harshly; the efficient checkpoint harness had to reproduce the audited runner exactly (200/200 rows) before its numbers were admitted. Two defects were found this way and fixed: a corrupted training-loss record and one claim of mine that was scoped too broadly. Full log: RESEARCH_LOG.md entries 26–31.
